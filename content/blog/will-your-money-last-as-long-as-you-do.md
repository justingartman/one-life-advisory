It's the question almost everyone asks first, and for good reason. Everything else in a retirement plan depends on it.

It's also a question without a single-number answer. Whether your money lasts depends on a handful of moving parts, and most of them change over time. Understanding those parts is the first step toward an honest answer.

## What goes into the answer

**How much you plan to spend.** Not a rough guess, but what your life actually costs, and how that may change. Spending often looks different in the first active years of retirement than it does later on.

**Where your income will come from.** Social Security, a pension, savings, investments, maybe part-time work or rental income. Each source starts at a different time and behaves differently.

**How long the money may need to last.** Planning around an average life expectancy means there's a real chance of outliving the plan. It's usually wiser to plan for a longer life than you expect.

**Inflation.** A dollar of spending today will likely cost more in ten or twenty years. A plan that ignores rising costs can look fine on paper and fall short in practice.

**Taxes.** What matters is what you keep. Which accounts you draw from, and when, can change how much of each withdrawal is actually available to spend.

**The order of market returns.** A market decline early in retirement, while you're drawing income, can affect a plan differently than the same decline ten years later.

## Why the answer doesn't stay answered

Markets move, tax law changes, and life doesn't hold still. A plan that made sense three years ago may need adjusting today, even if nothing seems wrong.

That's why this isn't a question to answer once and file away. It's worth revisiting at least once a year, and whenever something significant changes.

No plan can guarantee that money will last. What a good plan can do is show you the range of likely outcomes, where the pressure points are, and which decisions make the biggest difference.

## A place to start

If you'd like to begin on your own, write down three things:

1. What you spend in a typical year, including the irregular costs that are easy to forget.
2. Every source of income you expect in retirement, and when each one begins.
3. The accounts you've saved in, and whether each is taxable, tax-deferred, or tax-free.

The SEC's investor education site, Investor.gov, has free tools and plain-language explanations if you want to go deeper.
