For thirty or forty years, the deposit arrived on its own. Taxes came out before you ever saw the money. Retirement savings came out automatically. Whatever was left became the household budget, and the rhythm was steady enough that you eventually stopped thinking much about it.

Then you retire, and the deposit stops.

You may have a 401(k), an IRA, Roth accounts, a brokerage account, cash in savings, maybe a pension. Social Security may start later. Those accounts still have to be turned into a monthly deposit you can live on.

So when the paycheck stops, where does the money actually come from?

"We'll take money out of the investments as we need it" is technically an answer, but it doesn't give the household much of a system. Someone can be financially ready to retire and still feel uneasy spending because the portfolio has never been translated into something that behaves like income.

## **Start with what you spend**

A $2 million portfolio tells you what you've accumulated. The monthly paycheck still has to be built around what the household needs.

Before deciding which account to draw from, get a reasonably honest picture of what you expect to spend.

That usually includes:

* **Recurring spending.** Housing, food, utilities, insurance, transportation, and healthcare.
* **Discretionary spending.** Travel, hobbies, gifts, and helping family.
* **Taxes.** During your working years, much of the tax bill may have been handled through payroll withholding before the paycheck arrived. In retirement, you have to account for it more deliberately.
* **Irregular expenses.** Vehicles, home repairs, larger trips, weddings, or other expenses that do not fit neatly into a monthly budget.

Irregular expenses are easy to treat as exceptions, even though some version of an "exception" seems to show up pretty regularly.

The goal is a spending number close enough to build around, with a process for updating it as life changes.

## **Then look at income outside the portfolio**

Next, look at income that can cover part of that spending without requiring a portfolio withdrawal.

That could include Social Security, a pension, scheduled deferred compensation, rental income, or part-time work. Some of those sources are more dependable than others, so they may serve different roles in the plan.

Timing matters too. A couple retiring at 62 and delaying Social Security has a larger gap to fund during those first several years. Once Social Security begins, the amount the portfolio needs to provide may change considerably.

## **What's left becomes the portfolio's job**

Once you know what the household needs and what is coming from other sources, the remaining gap becomes the portfolio's job.

That gives questions about investment allocation, cash reserves, taxes, and withdrawal strategy a defined purpose. Instead of simply having a collection of accounts, you know what those accounts need to accomplish.

## **Decide which dollars fund which years**

Money you expect to spend soon has a different job than money you may not need for another fifteen years.

Near-term spending often calls for more stability because there may not be much time to recover from a market decline before the money is needed. Longer-term assets have more time and can generally carry more of the portfolio's growth responsibility.

Account type matters too. Traditional retirement accounts, Roth accounts, taxable brokerage accounts, and cash can have very different tax consequences when money comes out.

The right withdrawal sequence depends on the household's Social Security timing, healthcare coverage, taxes, liquidity needs, investments, and legacy goals.

## **Build the retirement paycheck**

This is the operational piece that can make retirement feel much more normal.

You can set up the retirement paycheck to look a lot like the paycheck you were used to receiving. A designated account sends a set amount into checking on the same day every month. Behind that transfer is a plan for how the account gets replenished and which assets fund it.

The household gets a predictable amount to live on without having to make a new investment decision every time money is needed.

The tax process needs its own plan. Federal tax can generally be withheld from many retirement account distributions, or a household may need to make estimated tax payments depending on its situation.

You also need to decide how much sits in the reserve account, when it gets replenished, which assets are used to refill it, and who is responsible for making those decisions.

A retirement projection can look perfectly reasonable on paper and still be difficult to live with if nobody has decided how the money gets from the portfolio to the checking account.

## **Where the money comes from can change the tax bill**

The amount deposited into checking and the amount of taxable income created can be two different numbers.

Traditional IRA and 401(k) distributions are generally taxable to the extent they contain pre-tax money. After-tax basis can change that calculation. Qualified Roth IRA distributions are generally free from federal income tax.

Taxable brokerage accounts work differently. If you sell $10,000 of investments, the tax result generally depends on how much of that $10,000 represents a gain over what you originally paid. How long you owned the investment can also affect how the gain is taxed.

Those differences can reach beyond the year's income-tax bill. Other income can affect how much of your Social Security benefits are taxable.

For households using Marketplace coverage before Medicare, income can also affect premium tax credits. Later, income can affect Medicare income-related premiums.

You'll sometimes hear a rule of thumb that says to spend taxable assets first, followed by tax-deferred accounts and then Roth accounts. That can be a useful starting point, but tax planning can change the sequence.

A household with relatively low taxable income during the first few years of retirement may choose to take money from a traditional account or complete Roth conversions during those years. That can use tax brackets that may become less available after Social Security and required distributions begin.

That's why I would want the withdrawal plan coordinated with the household's tax professional.

## **A hypothetical example**

Mark and Ellen are both 62 and retiring now. This example is hypothetical and only meant to show how the mechanics could work.

They have about $2 million:

* $1.2 million in a 401(k)
* $300,000 in Roth IRAs
* $400,000 in a joint brokerage account
* $100,000 in cash

They have no pension and plan to claim Social Security later.

They want about $9,000 deposited into checking each month for household spending. Taxes are handled separately in this simplified example, which is showing how the cash flow could work rather than whether this level of spending is sustainable.

### **Before Social Security begins**

For now, the full $9,000 comes from their assets. Their $100,000 cash reserve covers close to a year of that spending, and $9,000 moves from the cash account into checking each month.

Behind the scenes, they periodically refill the cash account from a combination of the brokerage account and 401(k). How much comes from each depends partly on that year's tax plan.

The household sees one predictable deposit. The planning happens behind it.

### **After Social Security begins**

Now assume Social Security covers $6,000 of their $9,000 monthly spending need. That number is simply an assumption for the example. Actual benefits depend on each person's earnings history and claiming age.

The portfolio now needs to provide about $3,000 a month instead of the full $9,000.

Their lifestyle did not suddenly change. The portfolio's assignment did.

### **Later in retirement**

Required minimum distributions eventually become another part of the plan for their pre-tax retirement accounts.

There may be years when the amount they are required to withdraw is more than they need for spending. The excess can be reinvested in a taxable account or used for gifting, charitable goals, or other priorities.

The deposit into checking can remain fairly consistent while the sources behind it continue to change.

## **Different households can build the paycheck differently**

Someone with a substantial pension and Social Security may need very little monthly income from investments. Another household may rely heavily on a diversified portfolio and periodically sell investments to fund spending. Someone retiring several years before Social Security may plan for heavier portfolio withdrawals during those bridge years and lower withdrawals later.

Some households prefer a larger cash reserve because having more near-term spending separated from the market helps them stay comfortable with the rest of the portfolio. The tradeoff is that cash has less long-term growth potential, and inflation can reduce its purchasing power over time.

Some households may also consider an annuity or another contractual-income strategy for part of their spending when transferring longevity or market-timing risk to an insurer fits the plan. That brings questions around liquidity, surrender terms, costs, inflation, taxes, survivor benefits, the insurer's claims-paying ability, and legacy goals.

Social Security timing, bonds, cash reserves, and portfolio withdrawals can sometimes address similar planning needs. The planning problem should lead to the tool.

## **Plan for market declines before they happen**

Taking withdrawals during a market decline creates a different challenge than experiencing the same decline while you're still working and contributing to the portfolio.

When withdrawals continue during a downturn, fewer assets remain invested to participate in a later recovery. The timing of poor returns can therefore have a larger effect once withdrawals begin, which is commonly referred to as sequence-of-returns risk.

A near-term reserve and some flexibility around spending can give the household more choices during those periods. The goal is to decide in advance how the retirement paycheck will operate when markets fall.

## **Expect the paycheck plan to change**

Social Security begins. Spending changes. Healthcare changes. Tax opportunities come and go. Required distributions eventually begin.

One spouse may outlive the other, which can change household income, taxes, Social Security benefits, and spending needs at the same time.

The retirement paycheck should be reviewed as part of the ongoing retirement plan so the way you're taking income continues to fit the life it's funding.

## **Make sure both spouses understand it**

In many households, one spouse has handled most of the finances for years. Both people should still understand the basics of how the retirement paycheck works.

Which accounts do we own? What gets deposited into checking each month? Where does that money come from? Who do we call if something happens?

Writing that down in plain language can make a big difference if financial responsibility suddenly shifts to the other spouse.

Turning a portfolio into a paycheck requires deciding what the household needs, which income sources cover part of that need, what job the portfolio has left to do, and how the money gets delivered.

Ultimately, the balance tells you what you've built. The cash-flow plan tells you what it can do for you.
