If you're a few years from retirement and you've saved well, you've probably asked some version of this: how much can we spend in retirement without running short?

It's a fair question, and it usually comes with a number attached. A percentage you read somewhere, or a figure a calculator gave you. Those can be a starting point, but they don't know anything about your life.

There's a question that has to come first, and many couples can't answer it off the top of their head. How much are you spending right now? Said another way, what does your current lifestyle cost?

For the hypothetical couple below, the answer turns out to be about what they spend today, rising with inflation, once the payroll taxes on their paychecks stop. Any room beyond that comes from planning. Here's how I'd get there.

## A hypothetical couple with $2.1 million saved

To make this concrete, consider a hypothetical couple I'll call Jim and Pam. They're fictional, and every number about them is an illustration, not a projection for anyone's situation.

Jim is 59 and Pam is 57. They'd like to retire in about three years, Jim at 62 and Pam at 60.

They've built up $2.1 million, spread across a brokerage account, IRAs and 401(k)s, and some Roth money. Jim earns $125,000 a year and Pam earns $60,000, so together about $185,000.

Their Social Security estimates are about $36,000 a year for Jim and $20,000 for Pam, if each claims at 65. To stay on the conservative side, I'm treating those as the amounts they'd actually receive at 65, with no increase before then, and rising with inflation each year after.

Plenty of couples in their late 50s look something like this. They've done a good job saving, and they still aren't sure whether they can stop, or how much they'll get to spend when they do.

## Start with what you spend, by working backwards

Before I ask what they can spend, I want to know what they're spending today.

The way I look at it is simple. There are really only two things you can do with money: spend it or save it. Taxes sit on the spending side.

So instead of asking what they spend each month, which almost everyone undershoots, I go backwards and start with what they're saving.

- **Jim's 401(k).** About $15,000 a year through work.
- **Roth IRAs.** $7,500 each, so $15,000 together. That's the base [2026 IRA contribution limit](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-66.pdf) (people 50 and older can add a $1,100 catch-up, which they skip), and at $185,000 of income they're under the income limit for married couples contributing to a Roth.
- **Brokerage account.** About $20,000 a year lately.

That's about $50,000 a year going into savings. If they earn $185,000 and $50,000 is saved, the other $135,000 is being spent: the house, groceries, travel, the cars, taxes and everything else.

> That $135,000 is what their current lifestyle consumes, measured rather than guessed.

This works for almost anyone. You don't need a line-item budget, and you don't need to remember what you spent on the car last spring. You need your income and what you actually save, meaning money building your long-term pool. Money set aside for a vacation or a new roof is spending that hasn't happened yet.

## Will that lifestyle keep going in retirement?

The next question I'd ask Jim and Pam is whether they want to keep living the way they live now once they retire. Usually the answer is yes.

That means the $135,000 lifestyle will cost more each year. I like to start with 3% inflation. We can fine-tune it, but it's a reasonable starting point. At 3%, by the time they retire in three years, the same lifestyle costs about $148,000, and it keeps rising from there.

You've probably heard that spending goes down in retirement. Research does show spending tends to change over time, sometimes falling as people age. But no one knows when that will happen for you, or whether it will. Some of the decline can be replaced later by healthcare or helping family.

So when I first build a plan, I keep the lifestyle all the way through and don't count on slowing down to make the numbers work. That doesn't mean you have to spend that much. It means the plan is built to test whether you could.

## Are they in balance? What the chart shows

Next comes the chart. It has two lines across their lifetime. One is the lifestyle they want, rising with inflation. The other is what their income and investments can support.

The question it answers: if they keep saving and spending the way they are now, under reasonable assumptions, can they keep this lifestyle going for as long as they need it? When the answer is yes, I call that being in balance.

Here are the assumptions for this hypothetical:

- **Returns.** 7% a year until they retire, and 4% a year once they're retired. I undershoot on purpose. You want a rate you'd be willing to bet your plan on, and if returns come in better, we adjust.
- **Inflation.** 3% a year, on both spending and Social Security once it starts.
- **Saving.** $50,000 a year until retirement, then none.
- **Taxes.** Inside the spending figure, as they are today. No tax planning, withdrawal strategy or Social Security timing strategy is modeled.

These are assumptions, not predictions. Nobody knows what returns will be, and they won't arrive in a straight line.

Here's how it plays out.

**Before retirement.** They keep saving $50,000 a year for three more years, and the $2.1 million grows to about $2.7 million.

**The first three years.** At retirement the paycheck stops. Before either of them claims Social Security, the investments cover everything: about $148,000 the first year, rising with inflation. That's roughly 5.4% of the portfolio, the heaviest stretch of the whole plan.

**Once Social Security starts.** Jim's $36,000 begins at his 65. Two years later, at Pam's 65, her $20,000 begins too. By the time both are claiming, that's about $58,000 a year together (Jim's has had two years of increases), rising with inflation.

**The gap.** The investments no longer carry the whole lifestyle. They fill the gap, roughly $113,000 a year once both checks are coming in. On a portfolio that's about $2.5 million by then, that's about 4.5%.

With no tax strategy and nothing fancy, just pulling from the investments under these assumptions, their investment assets last into their early 90s, to about Jim's 92 and Pam's 90.

That isn't the same as running out of money. When the investments are used up, Social Security keeps coming. It just covers only about a third of the lifestyle by then, which is why I'd want to keep improving the plan from here.

## So why are they nervous to spend?

If the numbers look like this, why would Jim and Pam be nervous?

Because they've been excellent savers their whole lives. For thirty years, every raise and every bonus had the same answer: put it away. Nobody ever told them when it's OK to switch.

So they think about what could go wrong. What if the market drops? What if one of us needs care? What if we live to 100? Those are fair questions, and a good plan should have an answer for each of them. The 95-year horizon below speaks to a long life, and the time segments later speak to a market drop. Care is its own planning question (what would pay for it, and which other goals would lose funding), and it belongs in the full plan too.

You can't talk someone out of the fear of running out of money. You have to show them.

That's why, in a first meeting, I'm not trying to solve every problem. I want to see where you are. Are you in balance, and can you keep going at the pace you're on? Once a couple sees that picture, the conversation changes.

## Why their spending may be lower than it looks

Remember the $135,000? Part of it is taxes they pay because they're working.

Payroll taxes for Social Security and Medicare come out of every paycheck, [6.2% and 1.45% of wages](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc751) for the employee. On $185,000 that's about $14,000 a year. Those taxes apply to wages, so they stop when the paychecks stop. They don't apply to IRA withdrawals or Social Security checks.

The $135,000 already leaves out their saving, so the payroll tax is the piece that falls away. Take it out, and the lifestyle they live today may take something closer to $121,000 a year to keep going, in today's dollars, before any other tax planning.

That difference changes the picture. I usually plan for a long life, to 95 for the younger spouse unless health or family history gives a reason to adjust. Under the same assumptions, at about $121,000 their investment assets last to about Pam's 96. Even rounded up to $125,000 for some cushion, they last to about Pam's 94.

So at today's spending, Jim and Pam are close to balance, and once the payroll tax drops away they reach it. That's why planning is where I'd look first, before talking about cutting back.

## How planning makes the same money go further

If a couple isn't in balance, we work on getting them there: which decisions need to be made, and what tradeoffs they're willing to accept. Once they're in balance, we look for ways to make the same money go further.

**Taxes.** You can't control what tax rates do, but you can plan around them. Which accounts to draw from first, and in which years, changes the taxes paid along the way. That can make a portfolio last longer. With a mix of brokerage, pre-tax and Roth money, Jim and Pam have choices to work with.

**Giving every dollar a job.** I think about this in time segments. Money they'll need in the next few years can sit somewhere more protected. That might be cash or short-term bonds, and sometimes a fixed annuity. Each of those comes with its own tradeoffs in access, cost, how long the money is committed and, for an annuity, the insurer's claims-paying ability, so I'd compare them side by side for each household. Money they won't need for ten or twenty years stays invested for growth.

This is where the first fear, "what if the market drops?", gets an answer. If the money for the next few years is already set aside, a bad year doesn't have to mean selling investments while they're down to pay the bills. The long-term money has more time to recover. A reserve buys time and options; recovery still isn't certain.

How much to set aside is different for every household. Some people want more set aside, and some need less. We test it with the numbers and with how you feel about risk, and see what fits.

**Social Security timing.** When each of them claims, and what the survivor would keep, is its own decision for a couple. I've written about it in [Social Security at 62 vs 70 for married couples](/blog/social-security-62-vs-70-married-couples).

These can make a meaningful difference, but they come after you know where you stand. A plan also does little good if you don't have the peace of mind to enjoy your life.

## How to run the numbers for yourself

If you want to try this for your own household, you need three numbers.

- **The money meant for retirement.** Not your emergency fund, and not money set aside for a new roof or a car. Money saved for the long term. For Jim and Pam, that's $2.1 million.
- **A return you'd bet your plan on.** One rate while you're still building, and a lower one while you're spending from it. I'd keep both conservative. If you hold cash, bonds and stocks, weight each by its balance instead of quoting your best account.
- **What you spend.** Take your income, subtract what you're saving and investing, and the rest is being spent. For Jim and Pam, $185,000 minus $50,000, so $135,000.

Then ask: can that lifestyle keep going, rising with inflation, for as long as you'll need it? Add your Social Security estimates, from your account at [my Social Security](https://www.ssa.gov/myaccount/), at the ages you expect to claim.

A spreadsheet will get you a rough answer. It won't test a bad market in your first year, a long life for the survivor, or how taxes shift once the paychecks stop, which is where a full plan comes in.

## So how much can Jim and Pam spend in retirement?

Under these hypothetical assumptions, about what they spend today, rising with inflation, once the payroll taxes on their paychecks stop. Their savings and Social Security look able to support the lifestyle they already have. Any room beyond that comes from planning taxes, withdrawals, time segments and Social Security together.

I see both sides of this. Some households are out of balance and nobody has shown them. But with couples who've saved well, the more common mistake I see is spending too little for years, because no one showed them the whole picture. That has a cost too. Those are years you don't get back.
