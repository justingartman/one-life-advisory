If you're in your mid-50s and you've built up around $2 million, you've probably started asking whether that's enough to stop working now instead of in ten years.

The balance is an important number, and at 55 it's also the easier part of the question. The harder part is timing. From 55, your savings pay for everything: your living costs, your health insurance until Medicare at 65, and the taxes on all of it. Social Security can't start until at least 62.

So the first ten years ask more of the money than any other stretch of retirement, and that's also when a bad market would do the most damage. Two couples with the same $2 million can get opposite answers depending on what they spend, which accounts the money sits in, and how those first ten years are built.

## A hypothetical couple with $2 million at 55

To make this concrete, consider a hypothetical couple I'll call Jim and Pam. They're both 55 and both thinking about leaving work this year.

They've saved $2 million:

- **$1.5 million in 401(k)s and IRAs.** This is pre-tax money.
- **$250,000 in Roth IRAs.**
- **$250,000 in a taxable brokerage account.**

They spend about $8,500 a month after taxes, before health insurance. There's no pension. At 67, Jim's Social Security statement shows about $3,300 a month and Pam's about $2,000, in today's dollars.

Every number about Jim and Pam is a hypothetical estimate for illustration, not a recommendation for anyone's situation. For assumptions, I'm using 3% inflation, a planning horizon to age 100, and a modest range of returns rather than one average.

Plenty of households at 55 look a lot like this. Most of the money is pre-tax, there's no pension to lean on, and there's a little in Roth and taxable accounts.

## Start with what you spend, not the balance

The first thing I'd want to know is the spending. $2 million at $6,000 a month and $2 million at $12,000 a month are very different retirements.

For Jim and Pam, $8,500 a month is $102,000 a year. Add health insurance, assuming they qualify for some help with the premium, and call it roughly $110,000 a year. That's what the savings have to produce in the early years, with nothing else coming in, and income taxes come on top of it. How much tax depends on which accounts the money comes from, which is a big part of what follows.

## Retiring at 55 comes in three seasons

I find it helpful to think of an early retirement in three seasons, because each one asks something different of the money.

- **The bridge, 55 to 65.** The savings pay for everything, including health insurance.
- **The gap, 65 to 67.** Medicare starts, but in this example Social Security hasn't yet.
- **The floor, 67 and later.** Social Security covers part of the spending, and the savings cover the rest.

They could claim Social Security as early as 62, which would lower what they pull from savings in those years. The trade-off is a smaller check for life. To keep the example simple, both wait until 67.

In the bridge, Jim and Pam are drawing about $110,000 a year from $2 million before taxes, about 5.5% to start. If you've only heard of the 4% rule, that can look uncomfortable. At 67, though, Social Security brings in about $63,600 a year between them, and what the savings have to cover drops to about $46,400 before taxes, less than half.

> A single withdrawal rate doesn't describe this couple. The heavy years come first, and that's why they need their own plan.

## Getting to your money before 59½

This is where retiring at 55 differs from retiring at 60. Most of Jim and Pam's money is pre-tax, and withdrawals from retirement accounts before 59½ generally carry a [10% additional tax](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc558) on top of income tax.

There are a few ways through, and the order you use them in counts.

**The Rule of 55.** If you leave your job in or after the year you turn 55, [that employer's 401(k) can pay out without the 10% additional tax](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-topics-significant-ages-for-retirement-plan-participants). There are two catches. It applies only to the plan at the job you're leaving, not old 401(k)s or IRAs. And if you roll that 401(k) into an IRA first, the exception no longer applies to that money. It's also worth checking how the plan pays out, since some allow partial withdrawals whenever you need them and others are more limited.

**Money you can reach any time.** That includes a taxable brokerage account and, in a Roth IRA, the contributions you made (as opposed to the growth).

**72(t) payments.** These let you take a set series of payments from an IRA early without the additional tax. It's another option, but the rules are rigid once you start.

For Jim and Pam, one approach is to leave both jobs the year they turn 55, keep both 401(k)s where they are, and use those plus the taxable account to fund the first four or five years. The IRAs and most of the Roth money wait.

## Health insurance before Medicare is priced on income

From 55 to 65, Jim and Pam need health insurance. For many early retirees, that means a marketplace plan through the Affordable Care Act.

The help you can get with those premiums is based on the income on your tax return, [not on what you have saved](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/federal-poverty-level-fpl/). $2 million in savings doesn't disqualify you.

For 2027 coverage, there's also a hard line. The larger premium help that expired after 2025 hasn't been restored as of November 2026, so the original rules apply again. A household with income up to 400% of the federal poverty level can get help, and the most it's expected to pay toward a benchmark plan is set by [the IRS each year](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-26-26.pdf). Above 400%, the help goes away completely. For a household of two, 400% works out to about $86,560 of income. Earning one dollar over that line can cost the entire credit, so it behaves like a cliff rather than a gradual phase-out.

So how could Jim and Pam spend $110,000 a year and still report income under that line? Not every dollar they spend shows up as income.

- **401(k) and IRA withdrawals** count in full.
- **Sales from the taxable account** add only the gain to income, not the whole amount.
- **Roth contributions** they take back add nothing.

By mixing where each year's money comes from, they can often keep their reported income in the $60,000s or $70,000s in the early years, while the taxable account lasts. As that account runs down, more of each year's money has to come from pre-tax accounts or the Roth, so the mix gets planned year by year rather than set once.

## Roth conversions and premium help pull in opposite directions

These same low-income years are often the best window for Roth conversions. Moving money from pre-tax to Roth while you're in a low tax bracket can lower your taxes and your required distributions for the rest of your life.

A conversion also counts as income. So the move that can save taxes over a lifetime can push a couple over the line and cost them their premium help that year.

I'd put both on one page, year by year. As an assumption, say the benchmark silver plan for the two of them costs $24,000 a year. With income around $70,000, about 323% of the poverty level, they'd be expected to pay about 10.22% of income, roughly $7,150, so the help is worth close to $17,000. If a $50,000 conversion pushes them over the line, it costs that $17,000 plus the tax on the conversion. And because the help is usually paid in advance based on estimated income, a conversion that lifts income above the line can mean [paying the credit back](https://www.irs.gov/affordable-care-act/individuals-and-families/questions-and-answers-on-the-premium-tax-credit) at tax time. That's a reason to plan it before the year starts.

Sometimes the lifetime tax savings are larger and the conversion is still worth doing. More often, I'd look at smaller conversions that stay under the line and save the larger ones for 65 and later, once Medicare starts. Actual premiums vary by state, age and plan, so the numbers need to be run for the household.

One more wrinkle: Medicare's income-related surcharge, called IRMAA, [looks back two years](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/2026-medicare-parts-b-premiums-deductibles). Income from 63 on starts shaping Medicare premiums at 65. It rises in steps rather than falling off a cliff, but it belongs in the same plan.

There isn't one right answer here. It depends on their tax brackets, where they live and how long the money has to last. It's a decision that touches taxes and health insurance at the same time, and I'd look at it that way.

## Plan for a bad market before it happens

What if the market drops right after Jim and Pam retire?

When you're withdrawing money, the order of returns makes a big difference. A drop in year two hurts far more than the same drop in year twenty, because you're selling while prices are down. So I'd test it directly: what happens if their investments fall 25% in the first two years?

The response I'd want decided ahead of time is a reserve. The next several years of withdrawals sit in protected assets, such as cash, short-term high-quality bonds or fixed annuities with a known rate for a known term (subject to the insurer's claims-paying ability and the contract's surrender terms). If the market drops, they keep paying themselves from that reserve instead of selling stocks while they're down. That doesn't mean years of money sitting in cash. A small piece covers the near term, and the rest is in assets that still earn something.

For a couple in the bridge years, with no other income yet, that reserve can be a large number. I'd size it year by year from what the plan actually needs, not from a rule of thumb. A reserve buys time and flexibility, but it can't promise the market recovers within that time. Knowing next year's paycheck doesn't depend on what the market does next month makes a difference in how people feel about spending.

Spending is the other piece. If part of their $8,500 is flexible, like travel or extras, they can decide in advance what they'd trim in a bad year.

You'll hear about a "safe withdrawal rate," one percentage that's supposed to make it all work. I don't let one number from one study run a plan. Those studies used different assumptions about fees, time frames and markets and arrived at different rates. What I care about is how this couple's money behaves on a bad path and what they'd do about it.

## Covering the essentials once Social Security starts

At 67, Social Security begins. Say $6,000 of Jim and Pam's $8,500 a month is essential spending: housing, food, insurance and the bills that have to be paid no matter what. Their combined Social Security of about $5,300 covers most of that, leaving about $700 a month of essentials for the savings to cover.

I like to compare the options the same way every time, with the same dollars going in:

1. **Portfolio only.** The savings cover whatever Social Security doesn't, and they stay flexible.
2. **A slice of lifetime income.** Part of the savings buys income that covers the rest of the essential floor for life, and the remaining portfolio handles everything else. Any guarantee depends on the claims-paying ability of the insurance company, and these products carry costs, limits and less flexibility.

The trade-off is usually flexibility versus certainty, and households land in different places.

Social Security timing is its own lever on top of this. Claiming at 62 with a full retirement age of 67 pays [about 70% of the full benefit](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/quickcalc/early_late.html) for life, while waiting past 67 adds [8% a year until 70](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/1960-delay.html), which can also mean a larger check for whichever spouse lives longer. That belongs in the plan because when one spouse dies, the smaller of the two checks stops. For Jim and Pam the example keeps 67, but I wouldn't decide any of it without seeing the versions side by side.

## The money has more than one job

$2 million also has to cover emergencies, larger shocks like extended care, and whatever Jim and Pam want to leave behind. If every dollar is busy producing income through the bridge years, it can't also be the long-term care fund, so part of the plan is [deciding which dollars are responsible for which job](/blog/retirement-portfolio-different-jobs).

## So, can you retire at 55 with $2 million?

I think of a retirement date as a set of conditions rather than a yes or no. For Jim and Pam, 55 looks workable if a few things are true:

- Their spending stays close to $8,500 a month.
- The bridge years are funded from accounts chosen year by year, with the Rule of 55 and taxes in mind.
- The health insurance and Roth conversion decisions are mapped year by year.
- There's a plan for a bad market before they need one.
- There's a plan for covering the essentials once Social Security starts.

Then I'd test 55, 56 and 57 side by side. Every extra year of work is one less year of bridge, one more year of saving and one more year for the money to grow. Some couples decide that trade is worth it. Others find they're in good shape to retire now.

In an example like this one, spending tends to move the answer more than the return or the balance. If the picture is tight, a year or two of extra work or some part-time income shortens the bridge.

The order of accounts, the health insurance line, the Roth decision and Social Security timing all affect each other, so changing one changes the others. That's where most of the planning happens.

> At 55, the balance tells you what you've saved. The first ten years decide whether it's enough.
