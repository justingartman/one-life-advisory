If you and your spouse are in your early 60s, you've probably been asked the same question from a few directions: do you take Social Security now, or wait until 70?

There's no shortage of answers. A calculator will give you a break-even age. A friend will tell you to take it while you can. An article will tell you to always wait. Each of those has a piece of the truth, and none of them is built for a married couple.

When married couples weigh Social Security at 62 vs 70, there's a question single people don't face. When one of you dies, the household keeps one check, the larger of the two. So the higher earner's claiming age decides more than their own income. It sets what the surviving spouse lives on, possibly for decades.

That's the number I'd want to see first, and it's the one the usual tools leave out. For many couples with one clearly higher earner, that points toward a split: the lower earner claims earlier, and the higher earner waits to build the check the survivor keeps. Whether that fits depends on your health, your savings and whether you can fund the years in between.

## How your benefit changes from 62 to 70

If you were born in 1960 or later, your [full retirement age is 67](https://www.ssa.gov/pubs/EN-05-10035.pdf). That's the age you receive your full benefit, the amount your work record earns you, with nothing taken off and nothing added.

You can start in any month between 62 and 70, so the choice covers a whole range of ages, not just three.

To make the numbers concrete, consider a hypothetical couple I'll call Jim and Pam. They're both 62. Jim was the higher earner, so his full benefit at 67 is $3,200 a month. Pam's is $1,700.

Every number about Jim and Pam is a hypothetical example in today's dollars, before taxes and without future cost-of-living increases, to show how the rules work. It's not a recommendation for anyone's situation.

Here's how their checks change with the age they claim:

| Claiming age | Jim | Pam |
|---|---|---|
| 62 | $2,240 | $1,190 |
| 67 (full retirement age) | $3,200 | $1,700 |
| 70 | $3,968 | $2,108 |

Claiming at 62 pays 70% of the full benefit, and that [reduction is permanent](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/quickcalc/early_late.html). Each year of waiting past 67 adds [8%, up to age 70](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/delayret.html), so three years of waiting adds 24%. After 70 there's no further increase, so in most situations there's little reason to wait longer.

From 62 to 70, the check is about 77% larger.

There's one more piece that's easy to pass over. The yearly cost-of-living increase applies to whatever check you're receiving. A larger starting check gets every future increase on a larger number.

Social Security is paid for as long as you live, it's adjusted for inflation, and it's backed by the federal government. For a lot of households, it's the only income they'll have with all three of those.

## What the break-even age tells you, and what it doesn't

The standard way to compare claiming ages is a break-even age, and it does tell you something useful.

Take Jim at 62 versus 70. If he claims at 62, he collects eight years of checks before the age-70 version pays a single dollar. For a long stretch, claiming early is ahead. Then the larger check slowly catches up, and for Jim, the two paths cross right around age 80. If he lives past 80, waiting collects more in total. If he dies before then, claiming at 62 collects more.

Every pair of ages has its own crossing point. 62 versus 67 crosses at a different age than 67 versus 70.

A break-even age measures one thing, total dollars, for one person. And it can't give you an answer until you guess how long you'll live, which is something none of us can know.

Instead of asking when the totals cross, I'd ask what the monthly check looks like at 85, 90 or 95. For Jim, that's $2,240 a month if he claimed at 62, or $3,968 if he waited, before any cost-of-living increases. That's the income that's actually there in your late 80s and 90s.

There are two things a break-even age can't see. For a couple, the second one usually decides it.

## Why a long life is the costly outcome

Waiting to claim protects against the most expensive outcome in retirement, a long life.

What strains a retirement plan most is living to 92 and still needing income, with savings carrying more of the load every year. Waiting to claim covers the case where you live a long time, and that's the case that's hardest to plan for.

For a couple, a long life is fairly likely. For a 65-year-old couple, the chance that at least one of them reaches 90 is roughly a coin flip, and higher if both are in good health. The [Actuaries Longevity Illustrator](https://www.longevityillustrator.org/) will estimate it for your own ages and health.

So when we build a plan, we plan for a long life by default. If life turns out shorter, the plan was conservative. If we plan short and it runs long, that's a much harder problem to fix at 88.

## How the survivor benefit works for married couples

For a married couple, the survivor benefit is usually what decides the claiming question, and it's the part I'd want every couple to see.

While Jim and Pam are both alive, they each receive their own check. At 67, that's $3,200 plus $1,700, so $4,900 a month coming in.

When one of them dies, the household doesn't keep both checks. The survivor keeps the larger of the two, and the smaller one goes away. It doesn't matter which of them dies first. The larger check is the one that stays.

So Jim's claiming age isn't only about Jim. It sets the income Pam would live on for the rest of her life.

Say Jim dies first. If he had waited until 70, Pam keeps his $3,968 a month. If he had claimed at 62, the survivor check has a floor called the [widow(er)'s limit](https://secure.ssa.gov/apps10/poms.nsf/links/0300615320): the larger of what Jim was receiving or 82.5% of his full benefit. Pam would receive about $2,640 a month.

> That's $1,328 a month more for as long as Pam lives, decided years earlier by when Jim filed.

Two details belong next to that number. Pam would need to be at her full retirement age for survivor benefits to receive the full amount, since survivor benefits [are reduced when taken earlier](https://ssa.gov/policy/docs/ssb/v64n1/v64n1p1.pdf). And starting the year after a death, the survivor usually files taxes as [single instead of jointly](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p501), which can mean more tax on a similar income.

So the survivor's picture is usually tighter than people expect, and the higher earner's claiming age is one of the few levers that improves it.

## What waiting costs: the bridge years

Waiting isn't free just because the later check is larger.

If Jim waits until 70, something has to pay the bills from 62 to 70. That might be savings, part-time work or Pam's check. Whatever it is, those years have to be funded in the plan, and I'd want to see that before calling waiting the better choice.

**Taxes.** The years after the paycheck stops, before Social Security and required minimum distributions begin, can be some of your lowest-income years. That's often a good window for things like Roth conversions, and waiting to claim can keep it open longer.

**Claiming early and investing the checks.** It's a fair thing to test, and the bar is high. Each year of waiting past full retirement age adds 8%, set by law, adjusted for inflation and paid for life. A portfolio would have to beat that after risk, while you're drawing from it, and an early market drop makes that harder.

**Working while claiming.** If you're still working before full retirement age, Social Security applies an [earnings test](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/whileworking.html). If your wages are over a set limit, part of your benefit is held back for now. That money is credited back as a higher benefit once you reach full retirement age. It does mean claiming early while you're earning a good salary may not put much in your pocket at the moment.

## The questions that shape your answer

So if break-even isn't the whole answer, what is? It comes down to a few questions.

**Do you need the income now?** If you're retired at 62 and Social Security is what makes the budget work, that pretty much decides it, and there's nothing wrong with that. Claiming early may be what makes retirement possible in those first years.

**What do your health and family history suggest?** Someone with a serious health condition, or from a family where people often don't reach their late 70s, has a sound reason to claim earlier. Someone in good health with parents in their 90s is looking right at the long life waiting is meant to cover. We can never truly know, but it's worth making reasonable assumptions.

**Are you married, and are you the higher or lower earner?** If you're the higher earner, waiting does two jobs at once. It raises your own check, and it raises the floor your spouse would keep. The lower earner usually has much less reason to wait, for reasons you'll see in Jim and Pam's numbers. If you're single, the survivor question goes away, and it mostly comes down to health and whether you need the income.

A couple more things are worth knowing. Full retirement age is a reasonable middle ground for someone without a strong pull either way. You can start in any month, so if waiting is right in principle but you need the income at 68, you can start at 68. And the choice is close to permanent: you can [withdraw an application](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/withdrawal.html) within 12 months by repaying what you received, or [suspend](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/suspend.html) once you reach full retirement age, but both are narrow.

Delaying the higher earner comes up often in our planning. It still isn't automatic for every couple, which is why we look at the whole household first.

## Jim and Pam's numbers, three ways

Here are Jim and Pam's lifetime totals under the same assumptions as before: today's dollars, no cost-of-living increases, before taxes. The totals aren't discounted, and they don't subtract what the bridge years draw from savings while Jim waits.

| | Both live to 90 | Jim dies at 75, Pam lives to 92 |
|---|---|---|
| Both claim at 62 | about $1.15 million | about $1.07 million |
| Both wait to 70 | about $1.46 million | about $1.17 million |
| Pam at 62, Jim at 70 | about $1.35 million | about $1.23 million |

If they both live to 90 and the goal were only the largest total, both waiting until 70 wins. The split, with Pam claiming early and Jim waiting, is still the one I'd look at first for them. Here's why.

Pam's check is the temporary one. Whichever of them dies first, the household keeps the larger check, which is Jim's. So Pam's own check eventually goes away either way, and waiting to grow it means growing something that may be set aside. Jim's check is the opposite. It's the one that lasts, the one the survivor keeps.

> Take the temporary check early, and grow the one that lasts.

The second column shows the case where the split comes out ahead of both of them waiting. If Jim dies at 75 and Pam lives to 92, the split collects about $1.23 million, compared with $1.17 million for both waiting and $1.07 million for both claiming at 62. Pam had income from 62, and she still keeps Jim's larger check for the rest of her life.

In the long-life case, the split gives up a small slice of the total in exchange for income now and protection later. The one condition is the bridge: they need to be able to fund Jim's years from 62 to 70. If they can't, claiming earlier is the honest answer there, and we'd plan around it.

## So should a married couple claim at 62 or 70?

There's no single claiming age that's right for every couple, because the answer depends on things you can only partly know: how long you'll live, what your spouse would be left with, and whether you need the income early for the rest of the plan to work.

So I'd frame it less as a guess about when you'll die and more as a choice about which risk you want covered. If your bigger concern is not living long enough to enjoy it, claiming earlier is a reasonable choice. If your bigger concern is outliving your money, or leaving your spouse with less, waiting may be the better fit.

For many couples with one clearly higher earner, the split is worth a close look: the lower earner claims earlier, and the higher earner waits to build the check the survivor keeps. The thing I'd be careful of is deciding it on its own, apart from taxes, withdrawals and what the survivor's plan would look like. It's one of the few retirement decisions that's very hard to undo.
