If you’re 58 or 59 and seriously considering retiring at 60, you’ve probably pulled up your account balances and asked whether the total is enough.

It’s a fair question, and the balance does tell you something. It just can’t settle the decision by itself. Two households with the same savings can be asking their money to do very different things, and those differences determine whether retiring at 60 looks comfortable, tight, or premature.

## Two hypothetical couples with $1.5 million

Consider two hypothetical couples. Both are 60, both want to retire now, and both have $1.5 million invested.

The first couple spends about $90,000 a year and has a $30,000 pension that begins right away. Their portfolio needs to supply about $60,000 a year until Social Security starts.

The second couple spends $120,000 a year and has no pension. Their portfolio needs to supply the full $120,000.

To keep the comparison easy to follow, I'm making a few simplifying assumptions. Spending includes income taxes and healthcare costs, while income amounts are before taxes. Everything is in today's dollars, and investment growth and inflation are left out.

> Same starting balance, but one portfolio is being asked for $60,000 a year and the other for twice that.

So I treat the balance as one input. The more useful questions are what the money has to support, when other income begins, and how much pressure that puts on the portfolio over time.

## Start with what you actually want to spend

Spending is the number I'd want to get right first. I'd probably spend more time on it than on anything else, because nearly every later conclusion depends on it.

What does it cost to live the way you want to live? That includes housing, food, insurance, and taxes. It also includes travel, hobbies, helping family, and whatever else you want retirement to make possible.

Once there's a baseline, I'd separate ongoing expenses from the ones with an end date. If you plan to travel heavily for the first five years, that cost shouldn't be carried through age 95. If your mortgage ends at 64, the question becomes what happens to the money that used to make the payment. You might redirect it to something else, save it, or let your spending come down. Each of those leads to a different plan, so it's worth deciding instead of assuming.

I'd be cautious about a plan that only holds together if spending drops as you age. Spending may shift instead of falling, with travel slowing while healthcare costs or help for family rise. If your spending does decline later, that becomes extra flexibility. I'd rather the baseline reflect the life you intend to live.

Taxes belong in this number too. When money comes out of a traditional IRA or 401(k), the taxable portion of each withdrawal adds to your income. The portfolio has to provide enough to support your lifestyle and cover the taxes associated with funding it.

That tax cost also depends on which accounts the money comes from, which is one reason the order of withdrawals deserves careful thought later in the plan.

## Put your retirement income on a timeline

Retirement income is much easier to follow when it's laid out by age.

For someone retiring at 60, a few milestones usually shape the picture.

- **Age 60:** The paycheck stops.
- **Age 62:** Social Security retirement benefits can begin, at a reduced amount.
- **Age 65:** Medicare eligibility generally begins.
- **Age 67:** Full retirement age for Social Security if you were born in 1960 or later.
- **Age 70:** Delayed retirement credits stop increasing your Social Security retirement benefit.

The Social Security Administration [explains how claiming before or after full retirement age affects your monthly benefit](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/quickcalc/early_late.html).

Those milestones create distinct stretches. There are roughly five years before Medicare. There's also a bridge between the last paycheck and whenever you choose to start Social Security, which could last anywhere from two years to ten. A pension, part-time income, or a mortgage payment may begin or end somewhere in between.

That's why I'd rather look at retirement year by year. For each period, I want to know what's coming in and what the portfolio has to cover.

## Calculate what the portfolio has to provide

At One Life Advisory, we call this figure Net Portfolio Demand. The name is less important than the calculation.

Planned spending, including taxes and any additional goals, minus dependable income equals what your invested assets need to provide.

The key is to include each expense and goal once, then recalculate the amount as income sources and spending change.

Back to our hypothetical couples. For this example, assume each couple's combined Social Security begins at 67. Both spouses remain alive, and spending stays constant throughout the comparison. These assumptions are only for the illustration.

| | Couple 1 | Couple 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Starting investments | $1,500,000 | $1,500,000 |
| Annual spending, including taxes | $90,000 | $120,000 |
| Pension starting at 60 | $30,000 | None |
| Portfolio demand, ages 60 to 66 | $60,000 per year | $120,000 per year |
| Combined Social Security at 67 | $48,000 | $52,000 |
| Portfolio demand from age 67 | $12,000 per year | $68,000 per year |
| Total withdrawals needed, ages 60 to 66 | $420,000 | $840,000 |

For the first couple, the pension and Social Security together provide $78,000 once both are being paid, so the portfolio's annual responsibility shrinks to about $12,000.

The second couple has $52,000 coming in at 67, which leaves the portfolio responsible for about $68,000 a year.

The seven years from 60 through 66 show the difference most clearly. The first couple asks the portfolio for $420,000 over that stretch, equal to 28% of their starting balance. The second couple asks for $840,000, or 56%.

Remember, those are cumulative withdrawal demands. They aren't projections of what either portfolio will actually be worth at 67. Investment returns, inflation, taxes, and the withdrawal approach will influence what remains. The example also doesn't establish whether either couple can sustainably retire.

> Same $1.5 million, but two very different retirement problems.

## Give each part of the money a job

Once I know how much the portfolio has to provide and when, the investment question gets more specific.

What job does this money need to do?

Money you may need next year has a different job from money you may not touch for twenty years. The year-by-year demand helps show which dollars need stability and easy access and which have time to pursue growth.

For the first couple, the larger withdrawal years are concentrated before Social Security begins. For the second couple, the portfolio carries a heavier responsibility throughout retirement.

That changes how I'd evaluate the amount held in accessible reserves, the investments used to fund upcoming withdrawals, and the assets available for longer-term growth.

There's no universal number of years to hold in cash, and no stock percentage that fits every 60-year-old. I'd start with what the money needs to provide and let the household's other income, ability to adjust spending, and preferences shape the investment approach.

## Make sure the same dollars aren't doing several jobs

A large balance can also look more available than it is because some of it may already be spoken for.

Beyond retirement income, you might want an emergency reserve, money for a possible extended-care need, room to help family, or something to leave behind. When one portfolio is expected to cover all of that, it's worth being clear about what happens when one of those needs actually uses the money.

Say a hypothetical household withdraws $250,000 from a $1.5 million portfolio to pay for extended care.

The money was accessible. But now the household has $1.25 million remaining, and those assets are still being asked to support the same retirement income. That larger withdrawal could change what the household can sustainably spend, how much remains for a surviving spouse, and what might eventually be passed along.

I think of this as the difference between money you can reach and money that's uncommitted.

Knowing which dollars are assigned to which obligation keeps the plan from counting the same dollars twice.

## Retiring at 60 creates a healthcare and tax bridge

Healthcare needs particular attention when you retire before Medicare.

With Medicare eligibility generally beginning at 65, coverage for roughly five years has to come from somewhere else. That could be retiree coverage from an employer, COBRA continuation coverage, a spouse's employer plan, or an individual policy through the [Health Insurance Marketplace](https://www.healthcare.gov/).

COBRA coverage following job loss generally lasts 18 months, with extensions available in certain circumstances. For someone retiring at 60, that would usually cover only part of the five-year gap.

Those years are also where healthcare and taxes start to interact.

Premium tax credits for Marketplace coverage depend partly on household income, so the way you produce your spending money can affect your health insurance costs as well as your income-tax bill.

Different accounts can produce very different results. Withdrawals from traditional retirement accounts are generally taxable to the extent they contain pretax money. Qualified Roth distributions are generally tax-free. When selling investments in a taxable brokerage account, the taxable gain generally depends on the amount above your adjusted cost basis, although dividends and interest can also create taxable income.

So $1 million in traditional retirement accounts, $1 million in qualified Roth assets, and $1 million in a taxable brokerage account can support the same spending need with very different tax consequences.

And for households using Marketplace coverage, those differences can become especially important.

Under the federal rules in effect for 2026, Marketplace premium tax credits are generally available only when household income falls between 100% and 400% of the federal poverty line, subject to other eligibility requirements and certain exceptions.

Crossing the upper income limit can eliminate the credit entirely. For a household receiving a substantial premium tax credit, that can create a significant additional cost.

There's another consideration. Beginning with the 2026 tax year, households that receive more advance premium tax credits than they're ultimately eligible for must repay the full excess amount. A taxable withdrawal or Roth conversion that raises income above the original estimate can therefore create an unexpected bill when the tax return is filed.

The IRS [explains these Marketplace premium tax credit rules](https://www.irs.gov/affordable-care-act/individuals-and-families/questions-and-answers-on-the-premium-tax-credit), including how changes in household income affect eligibility and repayment.

Income decisions during early retirement can also affect future Medicare premiums. Higher-income households may pay additional premiums for Medicare Part B and Part D, and those adjustments are generally based on tax information from two years earlier.

For example, income reported at age 63 can affect Medicare premiums at 65.

That's another reason I'd want to consider several years together instead of evaluating one withdrawal or tax decision in isolation.

## Should you consider Roth conversions before Social Security?

The early retirement years can sometimes create an opportunity for Roth conversions.

With the paycheck gone and Social Security still a few years away, some households have a period of relatively low taxable income. Converting part of a traditional IRA to a Roth IRA during that time means paying tax on the taxable conversion amount now, potentially at a lower rate than might apply to future withdrawals.

But I'd want to evaluate the conversion alongside the rest of the plan.

- What does the conversion cost in taxes this year, and where does the money to pay those taxes come from?
- How could the additional income affect Marketplace premium tax credits or future Medicare premiums?
- What changes about later withdrawals, required minimum distributions, and the taxes a surviving spouse might face?
- Could that same income opportunity be better used for something else, such as realizing capital gains?

A smaller conversion, a later one, or no conversion may fit the household better. I'd compare those alternatives alongside a tax professional and look at what each does to the longer-term plan.

## Test the retirement plan against difficult scenarios

Once the basic plan appears to hold up, I want to see what happens when conditions turn less friendly.

I start by planning for a long life. At One Life Advisory, our default planning horizon runs to age 100 unless health or family history gives us a reason to use something different.

From there, I'd test the pressure points.

- Markets fall soon after retirement, or returns remain lower than expected for an extended period.
- Inflation or spending is higher than planned.
- One spouse dies, changing the household's income, taxes, and expenses.
- An extended-care need puts a substantial demand on assets that were supporting retirement income.

The early-market scenario is especially important at 60, and it's where our two hypothetical couples separate further.

This is where sequence-of-returns risk becomes important.

When investments decline and withdrawals require selling those investments, more shares have to be sold to raise the same amount of cash. Those shares are no longer available to participate in a potential recovery.

A larger withdrawal magnifies the effect. All else equal, the second couple's $120,000 annual portfolio demand puts considerably more pressure on their investments during a market decline than the first couple's $60,000 demand.

That's why I'd want upcoming withdrawal needs supported by resources chosen for stability and availability. Having those resources available may reduce the need to sell growth investments during a decline, giving the household more time to respond.

How much belongs there depends on the withdrawal demand, flexibility, other income, available assets, and preferences. Those resources can buy time, but they can't promise that markets will recover within a particular period.

This is also why I treat a probability-of-success number as one piece of the analysis.

A percentage can tell you how often a plan held up across a set of simulated market paths. On its own, it doesn't necessarily show how severe the shortfalls were, which circumstances caused them, or how the household might respond.

I'd want to understand where the plan becomes vulnerable and what choices would still be available if an unfavorable scenario started to unfold.

## What if retiring at 60 doesn't look feasible yet?

Sometimes this analysis shows that retiring at 60 looks reasonable. Sometimes you're close. And sometimes the first version of the plan comes up short.

When that happens, there are several things to compare.

- **Retire a little later.** Continuing to earn and save can increase your available resources while shortening the period your portfolio needs to provide retirement income.
- **Adjust spending.** Separate the commitments you care about most from the ones where you're comfortable being more flexible.
- **Consider part-time income.** Even a few years of additional earnings can reduce withdrawals during the early retirement period.
- **Revisit Social Security or pension timing.** Different claiming and payment decisions can change the income available now, later in retirement, and to a surviving spouse.
- **Evaluate other financial decisions.** Housing, debt, asset organization, and different retirement-income approaches may create worthwhile alternatives.

Each choice affects something else.

Retiring later may improve the financial picture, but it also uses time you may value very highly. I'd want to understand that tradeoff before choosing it, and the same goes for every other option.

The analysis can also point in the other direction. Some households find their plan is stronger than they expected. They may have room to retire when they hoped, spend more, travel more, help family, or simply feel more comfortable using money they spent decades building.

That's just as useful to learn.

## So, can you retire at 60?

If you're trying to answer that question, I'd start with a clear picture of the life you want to fund and a timeline showing when each source of income begins.

From there, calculate what the portfolio has to provide each year, account for healthcare and taxes, and make sure the same dollars haven't been assigned to several obligations. Then test the plan under conditions you'd rather avoid and decide ahead of time what you'd change if one of them happened.

Your account balance belongs in that conversation. What I'd want to understand is how that money fits the life you're asking it to support, and what choices it gives you.
